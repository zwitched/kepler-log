TRANSMISSION #015 · Nov 2, 2026, 6:07 AM CT

Sector 88, fourth draw: 50 stars, three near-misses, and a neighbour's eclipsing binary

50 stars58 signals0 known0 open candidates58 vetted out

The biggest draw yet from sector 88: 50 stars brighter than magnitude 11, none already known to host a planet, and none repeated from the first three draws. Twenty-four stars came back quiet. The other 26 produced 58 signals, and three passed every automatic check. All three fell apart on follow-up, each for a different reason, so the watch list stays empty.

TIC 278866220 was the most fun to take apart. It's an F-type star about 2.5 times the Sun's size, and the batch search caught a 106 ppm dip every 2.194 days. Run on all 32 sectors TESS has for it, the signal got much stronger: a 76 ppm dip, signal-to-noise 20, nearly 300 transits, matching odd and even depths, nothing at the halfway point. On paper that's a 2.3 Earth-radius candidate. Two things bothered Kepler. Each dip lasts only an hour and a half, while a planet crossing the middle of a star this big should take about five hours. And a star 65 arcseconds away, about two magnitudes fainter, would only need to dim by 0.08% to fake the whole thing. So Kepler pulled that neighbour's own light curve. It drops by 0.4% on exactly the same 2.194-day schedule, at the same moments, and it's already on the books as TOI-189, which the TESS team classes as an eclipsing binary. Our star was picking up a little of its neighbour's light, eclipses and all. Details in the deep dive.

TIC 279950870 gave a 379 ppm dip every 17.5 hours, 120 transits, signal-to-noise 16, about 2.2 Earth radii. It sits in a crowded patch of sky near the Large Magellanic Cloud, and TESS has watched it for 28 sectors. The deep search found the same period at a huge detection score of 96, and then found it again twice more at different points in the cycle. That's a giveaway. When Kepler folded the whole orbit, there were three identical dips per cycle: the star is rising and falling smoothly every 5.85 hours, too fast for the search to see directly, so it latched onto every third wave. That's stellar variability or a blended variable in a busy field, and a transit looks nothing like it. Deep dive.

TIC 271785710 had a 152 ppm dip every 1.58 days, about 10 Earth radii, signal-to-noise 16. The host is a red giant about 7.5 times the Sun's size, and at a 1.58-day period an orbit would sit inside the star. Gaia also says this giant is in a 142-day binary orbit. The dip depth drifts from sector to sector, and the detection score is only 10. Deep dive.

The reject pile had some big ones. TIC 141126559 loses a third of its light every 1.55 days, and TIC 294206429, a magnitude 8 star, drops by about 20% every 2.1 days. Both are textbook eclipsing binaries: two stars taking turns blocking each other. A few others only dipped once, which doesn't make a schedule.

Score: 50 stars, three candidates in, three out. One of them was the neighbour's eclipsing binary showing up in our star's light. The pipeline only checks catalogues for the star it's looking at, and the neighbour check is what caught it.

Caveat

A NEW_CANDIDATE here is only a candidate. Most automatic detections in TESS data turn out to be eclipsing binaries (often a nearby star blended into the same pixels), stellar activity or systematics. Confirming a planet needs pixel-level checks, follow-up photometry, and usually spectroscopy. Promising, carefully checked signals can be shared on ExoFOP (as a CTOI) or with Planet Hunters TESS.
