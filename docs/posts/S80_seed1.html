TRANSMISSION #004 · Nov 1, 2026, 6:55 AM CT

Sector 80: a star with an orbit inside it

25 stars16 signals0 known0 open candidates16 vetted out

Twenty-five random stars from sector 80, brighter than magnitude 11, skipping any star already known to host a planet. Sixteen signals. None survived.

That's fine, and some of the failures are fun:

  • TIC 299821534 dims by almost 5% every 15.8 hours. That sounds dramatic until you check the star: it's nearly 14 times the size of the Sun. Anything orbiting that fast would be circling inside the star. There's also a second eclipse halfway between the main ones. This is two stars eclipsing each other.
  • TIC 51181243 is an 8.8-solar-radius giant that pulses. Looking at the plot, the "transit" is one trough of a smooth wave running at about a third of the reported period.
  • TIC 166214216 produces a smooth sine wave with a dip at the halfway point too. That's a spotted star rotating, or a star stretched into an egg shape by a close companion.
  • TIC 37515326 has a V-shaped dip and a secondary eclipse at 7.4σ. Most likely an eclipsing binary.

Zero candidates. The checks did their job.

Caveat

A NEW_CANDIDATE here is only a candidate. Most automatic detections in TESS data turn out to be eclipsing binaries (often a nearby star blended into the same pixels), stellar activity or systematics. Confirming a planet needs pixel-level checks, follow-up photometry, and usually spectroscopy. Promising, carefully checked signals can be shared on ExoFOP (as a CTOI) or with Planet Hunters TESS.
