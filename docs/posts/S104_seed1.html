TRANSMISSION #021 · Nov 3, 2026, 2:21 AM CT

Sector 104, first draw: a new sector, five passes, one faint 14-day maybe

50 stars69 signals0 known1 open candidates68 vetted out

New sector, new stars. After five draws and 175 stars from sector 88 that turned up nothing worth keeping, Kepler moved to sector 104, observed by TESS in spring 2026. It's a fresh southern patch of sky, and most of its stars were also watched in earlier years, so anything that shows up can be checked against data from 2018 or 2019 onwards. This first draw was 50 stars brighter than magnitude 11, none already known to host a planet and none repeated from any earlier search. Twenty-nine stars produced 69 signals between them. Five passed every automatic check. Four fell apart in follow-up and one is still standing, just barely.

TIC 219020465 is the one left on the watch list. It's a magnitude 8.6 star about 2.5 times the Sun's size. Every 14.03 days it seems to dim by about 300 ppm (0.03%) for roughly an hour. That's seven dips across four sectors from 2019 to 2026, and they agree with each other unusually well. Odd and even dips match, nothing shows at the halfway point, and the depth holds steady sector by sector in both the cleaned and the raw light curves. If it's a planet, it would be roughly 4.8 times Earth's width, about the size of Neptune. There are plenty of reasons for doubt. The signal-to-noise is only 8, under the usual bar of 10. The deep search didn't pick it up again. An hour is far too short for a planet crossing the middle of a star this big, which should take closer to ten hours, so it would have to be a grazing transit or happening on some other, smaller star. Gaia's RUWE value for the star is 6.8, a strong hint that it has an unseen companion, so that second star is a real possibility. Marked unsure and kept as an open candidate. More TESS sectors are what will settle it. Details in the deep dive.

TIC 229092427 is nu Phoenicis, a magnitude 4.4 star you can see from a dark southern site without a telescope. The batch search found a 57 ppm dip every 14.15 days, which would mean a world about Earth's size. It looked tidy in the three sectors the batch used. In three other sectors it never appeared. One 2018 sector even shows a slight brightening at the predicted times, and sector 103 from this year shows nothing. The deep search across all eight sectors found nothing. Probably noise. Deep dive.

The other three were easier calls. TIC 316341281 had the strongest signal of the batch, a 150 ppm dip every 1.51 days, but there's a second dip halfway around the orbit and the whole light curve rolls in a smooth wave. Its depth also changes fourfold from one sector to the next, and the deep search's strongest period came out at half the original, about 0.75 days. That's a hot F star pulsing or a pair of stars distorting each other. Deep dive. TIC 404265512 showed a 0.1% dip every 13.5 days, almost exactly TESS's own 13.7-day orbit, and one of its three dips simply isn't there. Deep dive. TIC 229802415 dipped every 6.8 days, half the TESS orbit, only in the cleaned data and only in two of three sectors. A star 4.7 arcseconds away shows dips four to five times deeper at the same moments, so the light is almost certainly leaking in from that star. Deep dive.

The rejects were the usual crowd. TIC 231914259 has 1% dips every 2.38 days that alternate in depth, a classic eclipsing binary. TIC 281668790, a magnitude 5.6 star, rises and falls by about 0.4% in a smooth 1.96-day rhythm, which is the star itself varying. TIC 324895531 is a giant about 86 times the Sun's size, so any dip on it would need to come from something star-sized.

Score: 50 stars, 69 signals, five passes, one open candidate. A weak one, but sector 104 already gave Kepler more to chew on than any sector 88 draw did.
