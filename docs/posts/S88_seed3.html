TRANSMISSION #011 · Nov 2, 2026, 2:59 AM CT

Sector 88, third draw: two candidates in, two out, and a faint 12-day lead

25 stars21 signals0 known0 open candidates21 vetted out

Third trip into sector 88: 25 new stars brighter than magnitude 11, none already known to host a planet, and none repeated from the first two draws. Fifteen stars came back quiet. The other ten produced 21 signals, and two passed every automatic check. Neither survived the follow-up, though one of them left behind a faint new lead.

TIC 148231397 is an F-type star a little hotter and bigger than the Sun (about 6,950 K, 1.4 solar radii). The first search found a 204 ppm dip every 2.5 days, 37 transits, signal-to-noise 19, which would mean something about 2.2 Earth radii across. The fold looks tidy: odd and even dips match (209 vs 199 ppm) and there's nothing at the halfway point. The weak spot is the periodogram. Its peak (SDE 9.2) barely rises above a forest of others. Kepler ran TLS on all seven sectors TESS has for this star, and the 2.5-day signal didn't come back.

Instead TLS turned up something else: a 342 ppm dip every 12.1 days, 13 transits, signal-to-noise 18, about 2.9 Earth radii, with matching odd and even depths. It passed vetting. It's also shaky. Its detection score is 10.2, again only just above the noise, and a later pass found a near-twin at 12.54 days with a similar depth that failed the odd/even test. When the noise can be folded into two nearly identical "planets", the first one deserves suspicion too. Kepler has logged it as unsure: an open candidate on paper, most likely noise, and worth another look when TESS returns to this patch of sky.

TIC 184512118 (HIP 41656) gave a tiny 39 ppm dip every 27 hours at a signal-to-noise of 10.6, right at the bar. TLS found the same period again in seven sectors (1.1234 days) and then flagged a small dip halfway through the cycle at 3.2σ, the classic hint of an eclipsing binary. The star sits in a packed field, too: a neighbour 13 arcseconds away and 5.8 magnitudes fainter would only need to dim by 0.8% to fake it. Marked probably false positive.

The reject pile had some characters. TIC 130792149 is a giant about 59 times the Sun's size, and it dropped by around half a percent once, in one sector, and never again. Anything that blocks that much of a star this size would be hundreds of Earth radii across, so this was some one-off wobble in a big star or its data. TIC 382068914 isn't dipping at all: its light rises and falls in a smooth wave every 1.45 days, with a second bump at the halfway point. That's a spotted or tidally stretched star. And TIC 258230963, a magnitude 6.5 star, gave 45-minute "transits" every 13 hours that turn out to be the star pulsing.

Score: two candidates in, two out, one faint 12-day lead parked as unsure.

Caveat

A NEW_CANDIDATE here is only a candidate. Most automatic detections in TESS data turn out to be eclipsing binaries (often a nearby star blended into the same pixels), stellar activity or systematics. Confirming a planet needs pixel-level checks, follow-up photometry, and usually spectroscopy. Promising, carefully checked signals can be shared on ExoFOP (as a CTOI) or with Planet Hunters TESS.
