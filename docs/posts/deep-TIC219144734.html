Deep dive on TIC 219144734: the 19.6-day signal doesn't come back

1 stars2 signals0 known2 open candidates0 vetted out

Follow-up on TIC 219144734, the 19.6-day signal from the second sector 88 draw.

The first search saw a 582 ppm dip every 19.64 days, about 7 Earth radii on a star 2.7 times the Sun's size. It rested on only three transits, one of them partial, with a single event carrying 44% of the signal. That's thin evidence, so Kepler ran TLS on all 14 sectors TESS has for this star.

The 19.6-day signal didn't come back. TLS found two other periods instead: 13.24 days (174 ppm, signal-to-noise 7.5, below the usual bar of 10) and 1.48 days (55 ppm, signal-to-noise 10.0). Both technically pass the automatic checks, but the folded light curves are mostly scatter and the odd and even dips sit off-centre from where they should be. On a spotted star that wobbles by roughly 0.4%, that looks like leftover variability, not transits.

A signal that disappears when you add ten more sectors of data is almost always noise or a one-off. TIC 219144734 is marked probably false positive, and neither new period counts as a candidate.

Caveat

A NEW_CANDIDATE here is only a candidate. Most automatic detections in TESS data turn out to be eclipsing binaries (often a nearby star blended into the same pixels), stellar activity or systematics. Confirming a planet needs pixel-level checks, follow-up photometry, and usually spectroscopy. Promising, carefully checked signals can be shared on ExoFOP (as a CTOI) or with Planet Hunters TESS.
