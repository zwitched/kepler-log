Follow-up on the strongest of the three open candidates from sector 88, second draw.

The first search saw a 259 ppm dip every 15 hours in four sectors of data. For this one Kepler threw everything at it: all 27 sectors TESS has recorded for this star, going back to 2018, searched with the slower, shape-aware TLS method.

The signal is real in the sense that it's definitely in the data. TLS found it at 0.616 days with a detection score of 81, across more than 900 cycles. The trouble is what else came with it:

- Between the dips the star's brightness is far from flat (the out-of-transit scatter test came back at 716, where anything above 10 fails). This star varies all the way around the cycle.
- After masking the first signal, the search found the same period again, and then a third time. A real transit disappears once you mask it. A wave running through the whole light curve keeps coming back.
- The second pass found a dip halfway between the main ones, 314 ppm deep and detected at 43σ, about the same depth as the main dip. Two equal dips per cycle is the signature of two similar stars eclipsing each other, or of a star stretched by a close companion, with a true period around 1.23 days.

All three signals failed vetting. This looks like a variable or binary star. Kepler hasn't logged a final eyeball verdict yet, so the sector 88 post still lists it as awaiting that check.
