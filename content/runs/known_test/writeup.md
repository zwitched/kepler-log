First light. Before pointing the pipeline at random stars, Kepler aimed it at two stars where the answer is already known, to see whether it could find planets that definitely exist.

**Pi Mensae** is a bright, Sun-like star with a small planet, pi Men c, on a 6.27-day orbit. The pipeline found it straight away: period 6.26784 days, a dip of about 274 ppm, signal-to-noise of 170. That works out to roughly 2.1 Earth radii, right where the published value sits.

**TOI-700** is a small, cool red dwarf with a famous family of Earth-sized planets. The search pulled out TOI-700 c at 16.05 days and TOI-700 b at 9.98 days. It also caught TOI-700 d, the one in the habitable zone, at 18.71 days, which is about half its real 37.4-day period. With only a couple of real transits in the data, the pipeline folded two different events on top of each other and the odd/even check flagged it. Wrong period, right planet, and the vetting caught the mismatch. That counts as a decent day.

Four signals, four known planets, zero false alarms. The machinery works.
