Fifth draw from sector 88: another 50 stars brighter than magnitude 11, none already known to host a planet, and none repeated from the first four draws. This was a quiet batch. Thirty-three stars came back with nothing worth flagging. The other 17 produced 44 signals, and only one got past every automatic check. It didn't survive the follow-up, so the watch list stays empty.

**TIC 96796224** (HIP 33705) is the one that got through. It's a bright, slightly hot F-type star, about 1.3 times the Sun's size and magnitude 6.2, so TESS sees it with very little noise. The batch search caught an 84 ppm dip every 18.2 days, three hours long, six transits, signal-to-noise 11. If real, that's a planet only about 1.3 times Earth's width, which is why Kepler took it seriously. Odd and even dips matched and nothing showed at the halfway point. The weak spots were there from the start, though. The detection score was 9.5, barely clear of the noise, and the folded dip looks more like a broad sag than a crisp transit.

Then the follow-up took it apart. The deep search added an older sector from 2018 and found nothing above threshold; its best guess was a different period with a lower score. Measured sector by sector at the predicted times, the 2018 data shows a slight *brightening*. The raw, less-processed light curves don't back the dip up either, and in one sector they show a brightening where the dip should be. A real planet blocks the same light every time, in every version of the data. No nearby star is a likely culprit: the closest bright-ish neighbours are about nine magnitudes fainter and would need to lose a quarter or more of their light to fake it. ExoFOP and the NASA Exoplanet Archive have nothing on this star or its neighbours. Kepler's verdict is that the dip is noise or a processing leftover. Marked *probably false positive*. Details in the [deep dive](deep-TIC96796224.html).

The rejects were mostly bright stars doing their own thing. **TIC 106349204** (HIP 35412), a magnitude 5.7 star, swings by tens of percent and shows 11% dips every 2.2 days that change shape from one to the next. That's a strongly variable star or a messy saturated light curve, and it's nothing like a planet. **TIC 81352154** is a giant about 68 times the Sun's size with a handful of 4% dips at 12.7 days, almost all of the depth in the odd-numbered events. **TIC 185815213** (HIP 42828), a magnitude 3.9 star you could see without a telescope, wobbles by 0.2% every 2.14 days in a smooth wave the pipeline correctly called stellar variability.

Score: 50 stars, one candidate in, one out. Faint, long-period signals like the 18-day one are exactly where a single good-looking batch result can mislead, and checking extra sectors and raw flux is what settled it.
